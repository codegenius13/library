# QUESTION 3: PRIORITIZATION OF CANDIDATE GENES AS POTENTIAL REGULATORS OF STRESS-RESPONSIVE METABOLIC PATHWAYS

## 3.1 Interpretation and prioritization

Yes. The candidate genes identified in the preceding analyses can be prioritized as potential regulators of stress-responsive metabolic pathways and proposed as targets for future functional validation. The differential-expression results provide evidence that several transcription factors and metabolic candidates respond strongly to cold, drought, heat and salinity stress. These genes are therefore promising candidates for investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying stress-responsive metabolism in maize.

The transcription-factor analysis identified 469 unique mapped candidate genes, of which 186 were significantly differentially expressed in at least two stress conditions. Several candidates were significant across all four stresses, including the ERF-family candidate **Zm00001d000339**, the C2H2-family candidate **Zm00001d013208**, and the HSF-family candidate **Zm00001d005888**. Their responses across multiple stress conditions make them particularly interesting candidates for investigating shared stress-response mechanisms. Other candidates, including WRKY-family gene **Zm00001d043060** and bZIP-family gene **Zm00001d030995**, also showed significant responses across the four conditions. Both upregulated and downregulated transcription factors should be considered because stress responses may involve activation of protective processes as well as repression of other cellular functions.

The integrated analysis also identified **65 genes that were significant across cold, drought, heat and salinity stress**. Several displayed consistent upregulation across all four conditions. For example, **Zm00001d020631** had log2 fold changes of approximately 4.34–5.63, while **Zm00001d047728** had log2 fold changes of approximately 6.35–13.83 across the four stresses. These expression patterns indicate strong and consistent stress responsiveness. However, their precise regulatory or metabolic roles should not be inferred from fold changes alone; functional annotation and further gene-specific evidence are needed to establish their biological significance.

## 3.2 Potential regulatory and metabolic targets

Transcription factors and metabolic enzyme candidates should be treated as complementary but distinct groups. Transcription factors, including members of the ERF, C2H2, HSF, WRKY, bZIP and NAC families, are suitable candidates for investigating upstream control of stress-responsive gene expression. Their potential influence on metabolic pathways could be evaluated by determining whether perturbing their activity changes the expression of relevant metabolic genes or alters metabolite levels.

Metabolic enzyme candidates represent a different class of potential targets. Their prioritization should consider statistical significance, magnitude and consistency of expression changes, and functional annotations connecting them to relevant metabolic processes. For example, **Zm00001d042107** was significantly upregulated in all four stress conditions and was annotated with the phytochromobilin biosynthetic process. **Zm00001d033222** was also upregulated across all four conditions and was annotated with the abscisic acid biosynthetic process. These annotations provide possible functional leads, but they should be verified against the underlying gene annotations before assigning definitive roles to the candidates.

The cross-stress candidates may be particularly useful for investigating shared stress-response mechanisms, whereas genes responding predominantly to one stress may be more appropriate for studying stress-specific adaptations. Candidates with incomplete functional annotations should not automatically be excluded, but their identities and functions require additional investigation before experimental selection.

## 3.3 Proposed functional validation

The prioritized genes could be evaluated through a staged experimental strategy:

1. **Confirm expression patterns.** Validate selected differential-expression results using an independent method, such as quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR), with suitable biological replicates and stress treatments.

2. **Test candidate transcription factors.** Use gene overexpression and loss-of-function approaches, such as CRISPR-based knockout where appropriate, to determine whether changing the activity of selected transcription factors affects stress-responsive gene expression.

3. **Assess metabolic consequences.** Measure the expression of relevant downstream genes and, where feasible, quantify associated metabolites, enzyme activities or other pathway-level indicators. This would help establish whether a candidate's perturbation is associated with changes in the proposed metabolic pathway.

4. **Evaluate stress-related phenotypes.** Compare appropriately controlled plants under relevant stress treatments, measuring suitable outcomes such as growth, survival, biomass or other stress-specific physiological traits.

5. **Investigate regulatory relationships.** For transcription factors with evidence of an effect on downstream genes, additional experiments, such as promoter-reporter assays or DNA-binding analyses, could help determine whether the regulation is direct.

Overexpression and knockout experiments should be interpreted comparatively because increasing or eliminating a gene's activity can produce different outcomes. For example, a candidate that is induced by stress may contribute to protection, represent a compensatory response, or be associated with stress damage. Its expression pattern alone does not establish which interpretation is correct.

## 3.4 Limitations and conclusion

The current analysis provides a basis for candidate prioritization, not proof of gene function or direct regulatory relationships. Differential expression does not establish causality, demonstrate that a transcription factor directly regulates a particular metabolic enzyme, or show that manipulating a gene will improve stress tolerance. Functional annotations also vary in completeness, so candidates with limited annotation require additional evidence before their biological roles can be assigned confidently.

In conclusion, the results support prioritizing selected transcription factors with strong, statistically significant responses across multiple stress conditions, alongside well-supported metabolic enzyme candidates, for future functional validation. Cross-stress transcription factors such as Zm00001d000339, Zm00001d013208 and Zm00001d005888 are reasonable initial candidates for investigating shared stress-responsive regulation. The metabolic candidates Zm00001d042107 and Zm00001d033222 provide additional leads for examining stress-associated metabolic processes, subject to verification of their annotations. Follow-up expression, gene-perturbation and pathway-level experiments would be required to establish their functions and determine whether they could serve as useful targets for improving maize stress resilience.
